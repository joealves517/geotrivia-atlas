For generations, Vietnamese schoolchildren learned a crisp geographic shorthand for their country's shape: the nation stretches from Ai Nam Quan to Cape Ca Mau. Yet a traveler standing beneath the arched stone gate of Ai Nam Quan today, known internationally as Friendship Pass, is actually standing inside the People's Republic of China. Situated in Pingxiang in China's Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, less than five kilometers north of the Vietnamese border town of Dong Dang, the historic fortress gate sits just north of the modern international boundary line.

This geographic quirk turns one of Southeast Asia's busiest overland corridors into a living lesson in shifting empires. On the Vietnamese side, National Route 1 begins right at the border near the pass and runs south all the way to Nam Can in Ca Mau Province. Across the barrier, China National Highway 322 carries freight northward through Guangxi into Hunan. Every day, fleets of cargo trucks and cross-border commuters funnel through this narrow limestone gap in the karst hills, linking Lang Son Province with southern China along a route that has carried armies and merchants for centuries.

Even the name of the pass has been rewritten repeatedly to match the politics of the moment. Built during the early Ming dynasty, the stone fortification was originally christened South Suppressing Pass, or Zhennanguan in Chinese and Tran Nam Quan in Vietnamese, a blunt statement of imperial frontier control. In 1953, as Beijing and Hanoi forged closer ideological ties, authorities softened the title to South Harmonious Pass. Twelve years later, in 1965, amidst the escalating Vietnam War, it was renamed Friendship Pass, Youyi Guan in Mandarin and Huu Nghi Quan in Vietnamese, celebrating political, military, and economic solidarity between the two states.

How the gate itself ended up wholly on Chinese soil traces back to the late nineteenth century. Following the Sino-French War, representatives of France and the Qing dynasty signed the Franco-Qing Convention of 1887, tracing a colonial frontier that placed the boundary slightly south of the pass structure. An 1894 map marked Border Stone No. 18 along the road leading from Dong Dang toward the gate, though that original marker was eventually lost.

When China and Vietnam normalized relations in 1991 and negotiated a definitive land boundary treaty, both governments agreed in 1993 to use the 1887 colonial line as their legal baseline. Because the two sides initially differed on how far south of the pass the line should sit, negotiators settled on a compromise enacted in a June 2000 border treaty, with full demarcation completed in 2008. While the settlement sparked debate among some Vietnamese commentators and diaspora communities who viewed the gate's placement as a concession, the crossing has since evolved into a vital commercial artery and even a rail gateway for the twice-weekly international express linking Hanoi and Beijing.
