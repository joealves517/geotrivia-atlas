Before a single drumbeat sounds, an offering is placed on the ground to placate local spirits and honor performers long dead. Only after this opening ritual, known as semah kampung, does the three-stringed rebab lute strike its first mournful note. On stage, the figure wearing the golden crown of a Malay king is a woman. Known as the Pak Yong, she leads an almost entirely female cast through three-hour nocturnal installments of song, improvised dialogue, and stylized court choreography that can stretch across several nights.

This is Mak Yong, a traditional dance theatre practiced across the Malay world, from Kelantan and Terengganu in Malaysia to Yala and Pattani in southern Thailand, and southward through Singapore into Indonesia's Riau Islands and North Sumatra. While many classical dances across the Malay Peninsula absorbed heavy influences from Indian and Javanese courts, Mak Yong's vocal melodies and musical repertoire remain distinctly native to the northeastern coast. Its stories draw deeply on Kelantanese and Patani folklore, yet they also act as a living archive for tales lost elsewhere, including Anak Raja Gondang, a narrative rooted in Buddhist Jataka fables that has nearly vanished in India itself.

A traditional Mak Yong troupe operates with strict theatrical roles. Alongside the female king and queen, palace attendants and male clowns carry the comic relief, while a chorus called the Jong Dongdang sings and dances between chapters. The accompanying ensemble is compact and hypnotic, anchored by the spiked rebab lute, twin gendang drums, and a pair of hanging gongs, sometimes joined by the reed serunai flute, keduk drums, and small kesi cymbals.

Yet those very pre-Islamic roots turned Mak Yong into a political battleground in its own heartland. Because of its animist opening rites and Hindu-Buddhist mythological foundations, the conservative state government in Kelantan banned public performances as religiously transgressive, keeping the art form off local stages for nearly three decades until the prohibition was finally lifted in 2019. Even before the ban ended, UNESCO had already stepped in, proclaiming Mak Yong one of the Masterpieces of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2005.

Today, survival hinges on a razor-thin human thread. Fewer than ten veteran Mak Yong masters remain active, and seasoned artists point to a sharp divide between commercialized urban revivals and the subtle, demanding discipline of rural village troupes. With few young apprentices willing to commit years to memorizing the epics and mastering the rebab, the future of this centuries-old royal theatre remains delicately poised between revival and disappearance.
