A single drop of rain falling on the crest of Wada Pass faces a continental choice. At 1,531 meters above sea level in Nagano Prefecture, this wind-scoured notch in the Chikuma Mountains splits Japan's water into opposing oceans. Runoff on the northern slope feeds the Shinano River toward the Sea of Japan, while meltwater on the southern flank joins Lake Suwa and the Tenryu River on a long descent to the Pacific. For millennia, human travelers crossing this divide faced an equally stark physical ordeal.

Long before feudal lords marched through the snowdrifts, Stone Age quarrymen climbed these ridges for volcanic glass. Deposits of black obsidian around Wada Pass and neighboring Hoshigato yielded blades sharper than steel, turning the mountain saddle into one of prehistoric Japan's most valuable industrial hubs. Archaeologists have traced broad arrowheads chipped from Wada Pass obsidian all the way to Kikonai in southern Hokkaido, roughly 650 kilometers to the north. Modern regulations now strictly forbid collecting the stone from the pass, leaving the glassy seams intact beneath the alpine soil.

_Figure 1: The historic Settai spring well, which once sustained travelers and pack animals along the grueling ascent to Wada Pass._

During the Edo period, the Tokugawa shogunate incorporated Wada Pass into the Nakasendo, the inland mountain highway linking Edo with Kyoto. Among all five official routes of the shogunate, no crossing climbed higher. The 22-kilometer stretch separating Wada-shuku from Shimosuwa-juku forced porters, samurai retinues, and pack horses up steep gradients exposed to blinding blizzards. In 1828, a wealthy Edo merchant named Kaseya Yohei donated 1,000 ryo so that its annual interest could fund a permanent winter relief station. From November through March, attendants stoked blazing fires and ladled hot rice gruel to keep travelers from freezing, while horses received boiled wheat throughout the year.

Geography also turned the summit into a natural fortress. On December 18, 1864, insurgent retainers of the Mito Domain known as the Tengu-to clashed in the snow with allied forces from the Suwa and Matsumoto domains. Using the precipitous topography and deep drifts to their advantage, the Tengu-to fighters broke through the blockade and marched onward toward Kyoto.

Modern engineering eventually bypassed the exposed ridge, though the mountain resisted easy passage. A motor road opened in 1925, yet heavy winter snows routinely severed the link until engineers bored the narrow Wada Pass Tunnel in 1933. Because that original bore is too tight for two-way traffic, drivers still take turns passing through in alternating single-lane waves. Today, Japan National Route 142 carries freight and commuters beneath the peak through the 14,777-meter Shin-Wada Tunnel, whose long-standing toll was finally abolished in April 2022, leaving the old cobbled switchbacks above to quiet mountain winds.
