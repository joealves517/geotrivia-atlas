At 830 meters above sea level in the Znepolje valley, a metal barrier and customs booth slice directly through the quiet mountain air along the Jerma River. Walk past the checkpoint on the western side and you stand in Surdulica municipality, Serbia. Cross a few paces eastward and you enter Tran Municipality, Bulgaria. Yet the street signs, family surnames, and weathered stone houses on both sides belong to the same settlement: Strezimirovci.

For centuries, this valley community existed as a single village, first recorded in Ottoman tax registers of 1451 as Stryazimirovtsi and two years later as Istrazumirofca. Following the Liberation of Bulgaria in 1878, it sat comfortably inside Sofia Province. That unity shattered in the aftermath of World War I. Because Bulgaria had fought alongside the Central Powers, the 1919 Treaty of Neuilly-sur-Seine forced Sofia to cede 1,545 square kilometers of predominantly Bulgarian-populated land to the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes, a territory Bulgarians came to call the Western Outlands.

![Strezimirovtzi Border Checkpoint1](/images/atlas/strezimirovci-inline-1.webp)
_Figure 1: The international border checkpoint dividing Strezimirovci between Serbia and Bulgaria along the Jerma River valley._

When international surveyors arrived to trace the new frontier, strategic military topography dictated the line rather than local geography. The commission ignored property boundaries, village roads, and riverbanks, bisecting twenty-five border communities across the mountains. Strezimirovci was cut clean through its center. According to persistent local lore, four desperate residents crept out under cover of darkness while the international commission slept and physically shifted the temporary boundary stones westward, hoping to pull as many houses as possible back onto the Bulgarian side before the final map was inked.

Even that nocturnal gamble could not spare the settlement from slow demographic collapse. Cut off from their fields, relatives, and neighboring markets by armed patrols and barbed wire throughout the twentieth century, families steadily abandoned the valley. In 1948, the Serbian side of Strezimirovci still counted 485 inhabitants. By 2002, that number had fallen to just 53 residents, nearly 89 percent of whom still identified as ethnic Bulgarians. Across the checkpoint, the Bulgarian side shrank even further, holding only 25 inhabitants by June 2008.

![Strezimirovtsi war memorial](/images/atlas/strezimirovci-inline-2.webp)
_Figure 2: A local war memorial in Strezimirovci commemorating villagers caught in the twentieth-century conflicts that reshaped the Balkan frontier._

Today, a sleepy international crossing handles sparse traffic between the two Balkan neighbors, while the surrounding hillsides remain dotted with empty homesteads. In Strezimirovci, a line drawn on a Paris conference table a century ago still governs where neighbors buy bread, pay taxes, and bury their dead, leaving behind one of Europe's starkest examples of a town hollowed out by a border.
